About

A bridge between health, communities, and technology.

My missionTo make health research and technology understandable, inclusive, and human, so the communities most affected can help shape the tools meant to serve them.

I'm a senior research project manager in the Intelligent Health Research Lab at Temple University's College of Public Health, Department of Health Services Administration and Policy, supporting NSF- and NIH-funded studies that use AI to address homelessness services and cardiovascular health. My role sits where public health, community voices, and technical teams meet.

I've been translating between worlds for a long time. As an EMT and then a medical scribe, I turned fast-moving clinical conversations into clear, accurate records. Global health research in the Dominican Republic and an MPH in health policy and management taught me how structures shape health, and why the people affected need a seat at the table.

As a first-generation college graduate, I know how much it matters when someone takes the time to explain how things work. Now I make sure that seat is real. When a technical team says "it's in Neo4j" on a community advisory board call, I make sure everyone also hears what that means and why it matters to them.

Terms often explained

Plain-language definitions of words that come up in community-engaged AI research.

Community advisory board

A group of people from the community a project serves who meet with the research team regularly. They review plans, ask questions, and tell us when something won't work in real life. Their input shapes the project rather than just reacting to it.

Knowledge graph

A way of storing information as a web of connections instead of rows in a table. Rather than a list of shelters, it holds the relationships between them: which one serves which need, where each is, and when it's open.

Community co-design

Building something together with the people who will use it, starting at the first sketch instead of asking for feedback at the end.

Social determinants of health

The conditions where people live, work, and grow up, like housing, food access, income, and transportation. They shape health as much as medical care does.

Institutional review board (IRB)

A committee that reviews research involving people before it can begin. Its job is to make sure participants are treated fairly, understand what they're agreeing to, and are protected from harm.

Focus group

A guided group conversation, usually 60 to 90 minutes, where participants share their experiences and opinions on a topic. It's meant to surface how people actually think, not to reach agreement.

Qualitative codebook

A shared guide for labeling what people say. It defines each theme we're tracking so that everyone analyzing the conversations applies the same meaning to the same words.

Mixed methods

Using numbers and stories together. Survey data can tell us how many people something applies to, while interviews and focus groups tell us why.

Predictive modeling

Using patterns in past data to estimate how likely something is for someone now, such as who may end up in the emergency department in the next few months. It gives a probability, not a verdict, and it can only learn from the data it was given, so who was missing from that data matters as much as the math.

Screener

A short set of questions people answer before joining a study, used to check who fits what the study is looking at.

Natural language processing (NLP)

Teaching a computer to work with human language: the words in clinic notes, survey answers, or transcripts. It can sort thousands of documents by topic or pull out every mention of a symptom, which is useful when there's far more text than a team could read by hand.

Human in the loop

Keeping a person in the decision at the points that matter. The system can sort, draft, or suggest, but someone qualified reviews it before it affects care or a person's access to services. It's how we catch the confident mistakes.

Agentic AI

An AI setup that carries out a task in steps instead of answering one question at a time. Given a goal, it can look something up, decide what to do next, and use a tool to do it. More independence means more can go wrong unnoticed, so these systems need clear limits and human review.

Large language model (LLM)

The kind of AI behind chatbots. It predicts likely wording, which makes it fluent but also means it can state something confidently that isn't true. Pairing it with verified data is how we keep answers trustworthy.

FAQ

Questions people ask me

These come up in advisory board meetings, at family dinners, coffee with friends, and in focus groups. Here's how I answer them.

How is AI used in health care so far?

Mostly behind the scenes, and more narrowly than the headlines suggest. The most established uses are in reading images, where tools flag possible findings on scans for a radiologist to confirm, and in documentation, where systems draft or summarize clinical notes so clinicians spend less time typing.

Health systems also use models to predict which patients may need follow-up, to route incoming messages, and to help people find services. Newer chat-based tools are being tested for patient questions and navigation, which is closer to what Dream-KG does.

Two things are worth knowing: adoption varies enormously between health systems, and nearly all of these tools are designed to assist a person rather than decide on their own.

Will AI replace doctors and nurses?

That isn't what's happening. What these tools change is where clinicians spend their time, usually by absorbing documentation and paperwork. Diagnosis, physical examination, difficult conversations, and accountability for a decision stay with people.

The more realistic concern is subtler: if a tool is wrong in a consistent way and nobody notices, that error scales. That's why human review and community input matter so much.

How do you keep an AI tool from making things up?

You give it less room to guess. Dream-KG is a good example: instead of letting the model answer from memory, it pulls from a knowledge graph of verified information about organizations, locations, and hours, so responses are grounded in real data.

Beyond that, you narrow what the tool is allowed to answer, test it against the questions people actually ask, and keep a person reviewing the output.

Is my information safe in a research study?

Research involving people goes through an institutional review board before it starts, and that review covers how information is collected, stored, and protected. Participants are told what the study involves and what happens to their data before they agree to anything.

As a participant, you can always ask what's being collected, who sees it, how long it's kept, and whether your name is attached. A study should be able to answer all four plainly.

Why involve community members in building an AI tool?

Because they know things the data doesn't. A tool can be technically sound and still fail if it assumes everyone has a smartphone, or lists a service whose hours changed, or uses words nobody actually says.

Involving people early also affects trust. A tool built with a community is far more likely to be used by that community than one delivered to it.

What does a research project manager actually do?

On any given week: recruit and screen participants, prepare IRB submissions, coordinate the team and its partners, run focus groups, analyze the data, and write reports for funders.

The part that's harder to put in a job description is translation, making sure the technical team, the community partners, and the funders all understand each other.
